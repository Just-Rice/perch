About Roost Connect

Privacy

Your PC, your business.

Roost is a way to use your own PC from your own browser. It has no accounts, no tracking, no analytics, no ads and no servers of its own. Here is everything it keeps, and everyone who could see anything.

Nothing collected

No sign-up, no cookies, no analytics, no tracking pixels. Nobody behind Roost learns that you use it.

Straight from PC to browser

Your screen, sound, clicks and typing go directly between your PC and your browser, encrypted the whole way.

Your PC decides

Every sign-in is checked by your PC itself: your passkey and the code from your authenticator app.

Who can see what

GitHub Pages, which hosts this website

The pages of this website come from GitHub Pages. Like any website host, GitHub sees normal web requests: your internet (IP) address, your browser type and which pages were loaded. It does not see anything you do after the page has loaded: signing in and using your PC don't go through GitHub at all. See GitHub's privacy statement.

Free public message services, to find your PC

Your PC and your browser are usually on different networks, so before they can talk directly they swap a few short "here's how to reach me" notes through free public message services (run by EMQX, HiveMQ, the Eclipse Mosquitto project and shiftr.io). They are used at the same time, so one being down doesn't matter.

  • The notes are sealed. Only your PC and your passkey (or your pairing link) know the key. The services can't read them, and can't change them without it being noticed.
  • What they can see: your internet address and your PC's, when the notes were sent, and a random-looking label that doesn't contain your PC's name.
  • Backup sign-in (your PC's Roost name and a password, if you turned it on): its notes are sealed with the PC's name only, which hides them from casual onlookers but not from someone who knows that name. That's fine: your password never travels at all (Roost uses a method called OPAQUE that proves you know it without sending it), and the PC still asks for your authenticator code.
  • These services carry anyone's messages and check nothing. Roost's safety never depends on them.

Address helpers (STUN servers)

To connect directly, each side asks a public helper (Google's and Cloudflare's STUN servers) what its own internet address looks like from outside. Those helpers see your internet address and nothing else: no names, no picture, no sound.

The connection itself

Once your browser and your PC have found each other, everything goes directly between them, encrypted with WebRTC's built-in encryption (the same used by video-call apps). Your PC sends nothing, and accepts no clicks or keys, until you have signed in with both steps.

The optional relay

If you set up the relay in the Control Center (for school or office Wi-Fi that blocks direct connections), the connection can pass through Cloudflare, using an account that you created. It stays encrypted between your browser and your PC the whole way, so Cloudflare carries it but can't see the picture, the sound or what you type. Cloudflare does see both internet addresses and how much is sent.

Your microphone

Roost never uses your microphone unless you choose "Talk through your PC" during a session and allow it in your browser. While it is on, your voice goes straight to your PC over the same encrypted connection and is played there; nothing is recorded or kept. A note stays on screen while it is on, with a button to turn it off, and it stops when you disconnect.

Guest links

A guest link you make in the Control Center lets whoever has it watch your PC's screen, with its sound, until its time runs out or you stop it. A guest can't click, type or change anything. The link is the key, so only send it to someone you trust; the Control Center shows while a guest is watching.

What is kept, and where

On your PC

  • In Roost's folder (%LOCALAPPDATA%\Roost): your PC's Roost name (like amber-otter-4821), a random ID, your settings, and for each device you paired: its passkey's public part (which can check a sign-in but can't make one), a label like "Safari on iPhone" and when it was added and last used. Also a short list of recent sign-ins and failed attempts, so you can see if someone tried.
  • Roost's secret keys and the secret behind your authenticator codes, in a file encrypted by Windows for your user account only (DPAPI).
  • If you turned on backup sign-in: a check value made from your password, not the password itself.
  • None of this is ever sent anywhere. Uninstalling Roost deletes the app, and asks before deleting this folder.

In your browser

  • The list of PCs you've connected to from this browser: each PC's name, the details needed to find it again and which passkey belongs to it. Remove one with Forget on the Connect page, or clear this site's data in your browser settings.
  • A few preferences: picture quality, "⌘ is Ctrl", the volume, where you put the Dock, your Key deck, whether connection details are shown and the PC name you last typed for backup sign-in.
  • Your passkey itself is kept by your device's password manager (such as Windows Hello, iCloud Keychain or Google Password Manager), not by Roost.
  • All of this stays in your browser. This website has no server that could receive it.

What you can see on the PC

While a device is connected, Roost's tray icon shows it, and the Control Center lists who it is. Only one device can control the PC at a time, and you can disconnect it or pause Roost from the tray icon.

Questions or problems

Roost is free and open source, so you can read exactly what it does in the source code. Ask a question or report a problem through GitHub issues. For a security problem, please follow the security notes instead of opening a public issue.
